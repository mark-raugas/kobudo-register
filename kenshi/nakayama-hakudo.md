Where [Kunii Zen'ya](kunii-zenya.md) kept his [Kashima Shin-ryū](../ryuha/kashima-shinryu.md) apart from the preservation committees, Nakayama Hakudō (中山博道, 1872–1958) is the same era's *institutional* preserver. A Kaga (Kanazawa) man who built the Yūshinkan (有信館) in Tokyo, he worked squarely inside the Butokukai and the modern budō federations while carrying classical form across the same decades — organizing iai as Musō Shinden-ryū (夢想神伝流), helping bring Shindō Musō-ryū jō (神道夢想流杖術) from Kyūshū to the capital, and teaching [Ittō-ryū](../ryuha/itto.md) — and is remembered as a rare holder of *hanshi* rank across kendō, iai and jō. He is included here for contrast, not completeness: preservation in the Shōwa period ran through institutions as much as against them, and Hakudō is the clearest case of a koryū custodian who took the institutional road that Kunii refused.

The *Shōwa Tenran-jiai* (昭和天覧試合, 1934), the record of the imperial-viewing matches held to celebrate the Crown Prince's birth, compiled by Dai Nippon Yūbenkai Kōdansha under Imperial Household Ministry (宮内省) supervision,
places Hakudō among the presiding *hanshi* judges — beside Nakano Sōsuke (中野宗助) and Ōshima Jikita (大島治喜太) — gives his line as [Shintō Munen-ryū](../ryuha/shinto-munen-ryu.md) (神道無念流), and records his birth in Ishikawa in Meiji 6 (1873; other references give 1872). ([kodansha1934tenran](../references.md#kodansha1934tenran))

His disciple Yamamoto Chūjirō (山本忠次郎), entered as a *senshi* (選士; imperial-match competitor), is singled out there as the foremost match-tactician among the capital's kendō teachers — but one example of the Yūshinkan's competitive weight.

According to the biographical entry in the Tenran-jiai, Nakayama Hakudō first studied the kendō of Yamaguchi Ittō-ryū under Saitō Michinori (齋藤理則), obtaining his license in Meiji 33 (1900); he then entered the school of Negishi Shingorō (根岸信五郎) to study Shintō Munen-ryū, taking the *mokuroku* in "the 26th year" (同二十六年目錄, as printed) and full license (免許皆傳) in Meiji 35 (1902). In Meiji 36 (1903) he received full license in Shindō Musō-ryū bōjutsu (棒術; so the source, rather than the character for *jō*) and in Tendō-ryū kendō from Uchida Ryōgorō (內田良五郎) and Takeda Kōhachirō (竹田幸八郎) of Chikuzen. In Taishō 5 (1916) he received a license in Ōmori-ryū and Hasegawa Eishin-ryū from Hosokawa Yoshimasa (細川義昌) of Tosa, and afterwards studied Ōmori-ryū and Hasegawa-ryū iaijutsu under 大森免久身 (so printed), taking full license in Taishō 11 (1922). He then established the Yūshinkan dōjō at Hongō in Tokyo. ([kodansha1934tenran](../references.md#kodansha1934tenran))

Other accounts add that he became Negishi's adopted son (其の養子となる) and that from Meiji 3〔6〕 to Meiji 38 (1905) he served as kendō instructor on commission (囑託) at Keiō Gijuku; neither point is in the Tenran-jiai entry.





Imperial and Butokukai honors he received included:

* Meiji 41 (1908), 9th month — awarded the title of kyōshi in kendō by the Dai Nippon Butokukai.
* Taishō 6 (1917) — the imperially-viewed martial arts match at the Fukiage Imperial Garden (吹上御苑), where he took part and served in the judging.
* Taishō 9 (1920), 5th month — awarded hanshi in both kendō and iaijutsu.
* Shōwa 2 (1927) — awarded hanshi in jōjutsu by the same body.
* Shōwa 4 (1929), 5th month — appointed a judge at the imperially-viewed martial arts matches commemorating the enthronement (御大禮記念武道天覽試合).
* Shōwa 9 (1934), 5th month — judge at the martial arts tournament celebrating the Crown Prince's birth (皇太子殿下御誕生奉祝武道大會), where he also fought in the specially-selected matches (特選試合).

Nakayama was appointed instructor at the Imperial University; circuit martial-arts instructor to the Metropolitan Police Board; kendō instructor to the Imperial Palace Police Department and to Tokyo City; martial arts professor to the Navy; and instructor at various other institutions.
