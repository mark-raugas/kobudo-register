Takayama Minesaburō (高山峰三郎, 1835–1899) was a [Jikishinkage-ryū](../ryuha/jikishinkage-ryu.md) swordsman of the Ōzu domain in Iyo, best remembered for defeating thirty-six of the Metropolitan Police's fencing instructors in succession — one of the provincial masters who, like [Okumura Sakonta](okumura-sakonta.md), made their name challenging the Keishichō across the great tournaments of the 1880s, and among [Matsuzaki Namishirō](matsuzaki-namishiro.md)'s recurring rivals.[^feat]

Born into a family of Ōzu-han Confucianists, he was taken to Edo as a boy, first studied Jikishinkage-ryū under Fujikawa Sadachika (藤川貞近)[^sadachika], trained further in two [Ittō-ryū](../ryuha/itto.md) lines[^two-itto-takayma], and entered Momonoi Shunzō's Shigakukan; later, in Kyoto, he became head instructor at the thousand-strong dōjō of Toda Isshinsai (戸田一心斎). In the Meiji period he served as a Shiga Prefecture police inspector (keibu, 警部) ([majima1996](../references.md#majima1996)).

In December 1884 he led the body of Kansai swordsmen the Shiga governor Koteda Yasusada brought to challenge the Keishichō, beating thirty-odd of its instructors (thirty-six in some accounts) in succession before [Henmi Sōsuke](henmi-sosuke.md) took the final stand — the feat for which he is chiefly remembered. He was beaten by Okumura Sakonta at the 1885 Kusunoki-kō tournament at Minatogawa Shrine, and his long contest with Matsuzaki runs through the accounts of the period ([hori1934](../references.md#hori1934); [majima1996](../references.md#majima1996)).

[^sadachika]: The Sōtairoku grave register for the Numata-han records Fujikawa Sadachika (藤川貞近, d. 1862) as a Numata-han samurai and swordsman buried in Bunkyō-ku — the same house and burial ward as Fujikawa Chikayoshi (藤川近義, d. 1798), whom it names 藤川派の祖, founder of the Fujikawa-ha. That places Sadachika within that branch, so Takayama's first training was Fujikawa-ha.

[^two-itto-takayma]: The two specific Ittō-ryū lines he trained in are not named in the sources.

## End Notes

[^feat]: The "thirty-six in succession" feat is the anchor of his reputation in the kendō histories; the parallel account in the Henmi Sōsuke record gives the number of Keishichō men beaten in the 1884 Kansai challenge as "thirty-odd"; the round figures are given as the sources give them. ([hori1934](../references.md#hori1934))
