## Summary

Sakakibara Kenkichi (榊原鍵吉) was a [Jikishinkage-ryū](../ryuha/jikishinkage-ryu.md) swordsman of the Odani-ha
line who bridged the late-Tokugawa military-training apparatus and the early
Meiji effort to keep swordsmanship alive. He trained under [Odani Seiichirō](odani-seiichiro.md)
Nobutomo (男谷精一郎信友), served as a Kōbusho instructor and later as head of the
Yūgeki-tai, and after the Restoration organized the gekken-kōgyō (撃剣興行;
officially-licensed public fencing exhibitions), the institutional link most
relevant to the Meiji transition traced in this book ([ndl-shozo-sakakibara](../references.md#ndl-shozo-sakakibara)).

## Names and readings

The formal given name (imina) is Tomoyoshi (友善), attested with reading ともよし;
the calling name (tsūshō) is Kenkichi (鍵吉) ([kotobank-sakakibara](../references.md#kotobank-sakakibara)). No azana
or gō is attested — the frequently cited "saigo no kenkaku" (最後の剣客) is a byname.
The kaimyō in the front matter rests on a single non-reference source and its
reading is unverified.[^kaimyo]

## Training under Odani; the yawara question

He entered Odani's Azabu Mamiana (麻布狸穴) dōjō in Tenpō 13 (1842) at thirteen.
When Odani urged him to transfer to a nearer major school — Genbukan (玄武館),
Shigakukan (士学館), or Renpeikan (練兵館) — he refused, and Odani conferred menkyo
kaiden (免許皆伝) on him gratis in Kaei 2 (1849), bearing the costs because of the
family's poverty ([sekaidaihyakka-jiki](../references.md#sekaidaihyakka-jiki)). His sword training was
Jikishinkage-ryū alone. He did, however, learn [Totsuka-ha Yōshin-ryū](../ryuha/totsuka-ryu.md) (戸塚派楊心流)
jūjutsu at the same dōjō, taught by the Numazu-han instructors Kashiwazaki
Matashirō (柏崎又四郎) and Aizawa Katsuyuki (藍澤勝之). That the Odani dōjō imported
jūjutsu instruction rather than transmitting its own is the evidentiary point: it
suggests Jikishinkage-ryū itself retained little yawara (柔; grappling) in its
curriculum by mid-century — an inference developed below. ([kotobank-totsuka](../references.md#kotobank-totsuka); [chiba-totsuka-grave](../references.md#chiba-totsuka-grave))

## Fellow disciples

Laterally, through Odani rather than his own dōjō, he sits beside Shimada
Toranosuke (島田虎之助; Katsu Kaishū's sword teacher) ([kotobank-shimada](../references.md#kotobank-shimada)),
[Mitsuhashi Torazō](mitsuhashi-torazo.md) (三橋虎蔵), Yokokawa Shichirō (横川七郎), and Amano Hachirō
(天野八郎; the Shōgi-tai leader) ([watanabe-eimeiroku](../references.md#watanabe-eimeiroku))([wikipedia-sakakibara-circle](../references.md#wikipedia-sakakibara-circle)).

## Kōbusho service and bakumatsu career

Odani recommended him as a founding Kōbusho kenjutsu instructor in Ansei 3 (1856);
the academy opened under Odani as tōdori and was abolished in the eleventh month
of Keiō 2 (1866), absorbed into the Rikugunsho (陸軍所). The reference dictionaries
give his grade as kyōju-kata (教授方) rising to shihan-yaku nami (師範役並) with
ryō-goban jōseki (両御番上席) in Genji 1 (1864).[^kobusho] He became Tokugawa
Iemochi's personal fencing instructor and rose to ninomaru rusui-yaku kaku
(二ノ丸留守居役格).

He accompanied the shōgun's Kyoto procession in 1863 (reportedly cutting down Tosa
rōnin at Shijō-gawara), was in the Kansai for the Chōshū campaign in 1865, and
returned to Edo when Iemochi died at Osaka Castle in the seventh month of Keiō 2
(1866) ([nagakura-shinsengumi](../references.md#nagakura-shinsengumi))([jawiki_sakakibara](../references.md#jawiki_sakakibara)). Transferred to Yūgeki-tai tōdori (遊撃隊頭取)
under the military reform, he soon resigned and opened a dōjō at his Shitaya
Kurumazaka (下谷車坂) residence ([ndl-shozo-sakakibara](../references.md#ndl-shozo-sakakibara)). At Ueno in 1868 he
did not join the Shōgi-tai ([japanknowledge-shogitai](../references.md#japanknowledge-shogitai)) but escorted the Rinnōji-no-miya prince Kōgen
(輪王寺宮公現入道親王, later Kitashirakawa-no-miya Yoshihisa) to safety at Mikawajima
(三河島), carrying him by turns with the bathhouse-keeper Echizen-ya Sahei
(越前屋佐兵衛). He then followed Tokugawa Iesato to Sunpu (later Shizuoka-han),
returned to Tokyo in Meiji 3 (1870), and declined the Gyōbushō daikeibu
(刑部省大警部) post, recommending his brother Ōsawa Tetsusaburō (大沢鉄三郎) instead.

## Teaching

Sakakibara's time in the early 1860s was spent teaching at the Kōbusho. He held no private dōjō during his shogunal-guard years; his own dōjō at Kurumazaka dates from the eleventh month of Keiō 2 (1866), when the Kōbusho was reorganized into the Rikugunsho ([jawiki_sakakibara](../references.md#jawiki_sakakibara)).

Before 1866, Sakakibara's teaching platform was his Kōbusho office (kyōju-kata 教授方, later shihan-yaku 師範役) itself, not a private school.  After 1866 he had his own dōjō, though his shogunal service was not yet over.

The Jikishinkage-ryū private dōjō of that earlier time was not Sakakibara's but instead Odani Seiichirō's (男谷精一郎). In Odani's absences it was run by his shihan-dai (師範代) [Honme Yarijirō](honme-yarijiro.md) (本目鑓次郎) — himself a Kōbusho Jikishinkage-ryū kyōju-kata level instructor. Odani's dōjō was at Azabu Mamiana from its founding in Bunsei 6 (1823) until Ansei 5 (1858), when, having been given his late teacher Danno Shinpansai's dōjō at Honjo Kamezawa-chō (本所亀沢町), he moved there.

Through the Kōbusho years the Odani dōjō — the Honjo dōjō that Britannica calls the most prestigious of the bakumatsu, producing Shimada Toranosuke, Mitsuhashi Torazō, Yokokawa Shichirō, and Sakakibara — was Odani's own school. Sakakibara's came later. ([nakamura1996denpa1](../references.md#nakamura1996denpa1); [nakamura1999denpa](../references.md#nakamura1999denpa))

## Gekken-kōgyō

In Meiji 6 (1873) he founded the officially-licensed gekken-kōgyō at Asakusa
Saemon-gashi (浅草左衛門河岸), backed by the Tokyo governor Ōkubo Ichiō (大久保一翁)
and modeled on sumō staging — a board ring, east/west sides, three-bout matches, a
referee. It was hugely popular until a glut of imitators drew a Tokyo ban that July
([kotobank-sakakibara](../references.md#kotobank-sakakibara); [nakamura-kendo-jiten](../references.md#nakamura-kendo-jiten)).

## Kabuto-wari

At the imperial-viewing kabuto-wari (兜割り; helmet-cutting) held in 1886 or 1887 at
the Fushimi-no-miya (伏見宮) residence, three men attempted to cut a metal helmet
before Emperor Meiji: [Henmi Sōsuke](henmi-sosuke.md) (逸見宗助) and [Ueda Umanosuke](ueda-umanosuke.md) (上田馬之助), both
Shigakukan (士学館) men, failed; Sakakibara cut roughly san-zun go-bu (三寸五分;
≈ 10.6 cm) into the helmet with a Dōtanuki (同田貫) blade
([kotobank-sakakibara](../references.md#kotobank-sakakibara); [sekaidaihyakka-sakakibara](../references.md#sekaidaihyakka-sakakibara)).[^kabuto]

## Students, transmission, and succession

The documented inner circle of Sakakibara is small despite the scale of the Kurumazaka dōjō and
the gekken-kōgyō.

[Yamada Jirōkichi](yamada-jirokichi.md) (山田次朗吉, 1863–1929; art name Ittokusai 一徳斎)
is the successor: on New Year's Day Meiji 27 (1894) Sakakibara conferred menkyo
kaiden on him as 15th-generation head and handed over the dōjō, dying that
September ([jawiki_sakakibara](../references.md#jawiki_sakakibara)). Yamada also trained kata under the
Fujikawa-ha (藤川派); a second teacher is variously given as "Yamada Hachirō"
([yamada1931ittokusai](../references.md#yamada1931ittokusai)) or Saitō Akinobu (斎藤鑑信) ([ishigaki-gokui-denkai](../references.md#ishigaki-gokui-denkai)).

Takeda Sōkaku (武田惣角) is claimed by family tradition
as an uchi-deshi, but this is weakly corroborated and his sword work is more
plausibly Ono-ha Ittō-ryū (小野派一刀流); recorded here as contested, not attested
transmission — his life and Daitō-ryū transmission are treated definitively by Ellis Amdur and the late Stanley Pranin ([ikezuki_takeda](../references.md#ikezuki_takeda); [amdur2018banquo](../references.md#amdur2018banquo); [amdur2018hidden](../references.md#amdur2018hidden); [amdur2014old](../references.md#amdur2014old); [amdur2016dueling](../references.md#amdur2016dueling); [Pranin1996Daitoryu](../references.md#Pranin1996Daitoryu)).[^furi]

By the main-line reckoning Sakakibara is the 14th head, and the line was very
likely *almost* broken at his death — the natural successor would have been Shimada
Toranosuke, who predeceased him.  Yamada Jirōkichi carried the line forward.[^iwasa]

## Jigeiko Focus

His later dōjō was known for sparring — jigeiko and shiai — with only periodic kata
practice, as the MFA's *Gekkenka* print attests ([mfa-gekkenka](../references.md#mfa-gekkenka)).

He had been a subject of the print trade far earlier, in his Kōbusho years. The
*nishiki-e* series *Taihei eiyū kagami* (泰平英雄鏡) portrayed the academy's
instructors as warriors of the Genpei wars, and drew Sakakibara as Nei Daiyata,
in a set of five — alongside Odani Seiichirō, [Matsudaira Chikaranosuke](matsudaira-chikaranosuke.md),
Mitsuhashi Torazō and Honda Tetsujirō — that sold eight hundred sheets before
the authorities seized the blocks and questioned the publisher.[^taihei-sk]

[^taihei-sk]: *Tōkyō shishi gaihen: Kōbusho* (東京市史外編, 講武所) (NDL 1225131, image 54, printed pp. 42–43).

At this time Jikishinkage-ryū was chiefly a sword art increasingly focused on shiai. Odani was also licensed in [Hōzōin-ryū](../ryuha/hozoin-ryu.md) sōjutsu (槍術; spearmanship), Shimada Toranosuke practiced [Kitō-ryū](../ryuha/kito-ryu.md) jūjutsu, and both Odani and Sakakibara took Totsuka-ha Yōshin-ryū at Odani's dōjō.[^namiki]

One line of contemporary practice has an old schedule from Sakakibara's dōjō that lists *hōjō* (法定) practice as occurring once a month.

## Death and burial

He died 11 September 1894 (Meiji 27) of kakke shōshin (脚気衝心; beriberi heart failure), kyōnen 65, and was
buried at Yotsuya Saiō-ji (四谷西応寺); the grave is a Shinjuku-ku designated
historic site.
