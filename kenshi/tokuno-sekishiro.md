Tokunō Sekishirō (得能関四郎, 1842–1908; imina Michihisa 通久) was a Keishichō gekken instructor who received both the first seirenshō and the first hanshi title from the Dai Nippon Butokukai, and was reckoned the foremost of the "three elders" of the Tokyo kendō world. His swordsmanship was [Jikishinkage-ryū](../ryuha/jikishinkage-ryu.md) of the Naganuma line — which places a second Naganuma-ha figure, after Kawaji, at the heart of the Meiji police layer.[^naganuma]

Born the son of a samurai of the Numata domain, he entered at fifteen (1856) the Shiba-Atagoshita dōjō of the domain's Jikishinkage-ryū instructor Naganuma Masasato (長沼恂郷, also written 恂教; gō Shōbee, 笑兵衛; d. 1865), took full transmission (menkyo kaiden) at twenty-one in 1862, and after his teacher's death studied under the son, Kashōjin (可笑人), serving as juku-head of the Naganuma dōjō. After the abolition of the domains he became a city warden in Tokyo, and in 1880 was taken into the Keishikyoku as a guard-officer (警衛掛); as a gekken sewakari he joined in compiling the Keishi-ryū ([jawiki_tokuno](../references.md#jawiki_tokuno); [jawiki_keishiryu](../references.md#jawiki_keishiryu)). His match record ran strong through the 1880s, including an 1882 imperial-review win. He met [Matsuzaki Namishirō](matsuzaki-namishiro.md) twice in the mid-1880s — a draw at the Imperial Household Ministry's Saineikan and a win over him at the Mukōgaoka Yayoi-sha tournament (c. 1884–1885); no source records the reverse result.[^yayoi]

At the first Butokusai great embukai in 1895 he defeated [Okumura Sakonta](okumura-sakonta.md) and was awarded the first seirenshō (精錬証); across the following annual meetings he was never beaten, and in 1903 he received the Butokukai's first hanshi (範士) title ([jawiki_tokuno](../references.md#jawiki_tokuno); [jawiki_butokukai](../references.md#jawiki_butokukai)). In his last years he was named foremost of the "three elders of the Tokyo kendō world" (東都剣道界の三元老), the other two being [Shingai Tadaatsu](shingai-tadaatsu.md) (真貝忠篤) and Negishi Shingorō. On an early morning in July 1908 he took his own life at home by cutting his throat — leaving no note, the act was ascribed to the stroke he had suffered that spring — at the age of sixty-seven.[^seppuku]

The son he continued under, Kashōjin, is Naganuma Michisato (称郷, d. 1895), last head of the 正兵衛家 branch. This identifies Tokunō directly with that branch-house line as both pupil and successor.[^naganuma-kanji]

[^naganuma-kanji]: The Sōtairoku grave register for the Numata-han gives 恂郷 as Masasato (d. 1865) and 称郷 as Michisato (d. 1895), which fits this career — the first teacher dying three years after the menkyo kaiden. 恂教 is an attested alternative writing of the first name.

The Keishichō put [Sakakibara Kenkichi](sakakibara-kenkichi.md) in charge of vetting the first *gekken sewakari* recruits (1879), and the intake of those first years — [Shimoe Hidetarō](shimoe-hidetaro.md) in 1879, Tokunō in 1880 — went on to codify the Keishi-ryū *gekken* kata.

## End Notes

[^yayoi]: The tournament is dated here as a range because the secondary literature disagrees on the dating and numbering of the Yayoi-sha meets (the same tournament is called both the 2nd and the 3rd, and is placed in 1884), and the Keishichō budō history separately calls the 1885 Yayoi meet "the first national kenjutsu tournament," which cannot be squared with 1884 already being a *zenkoku* (全国) event. That Okumura Sakonta's record lists five individually named opponents at this one meet (one loss, one draw, three wins) shows it was decided by direct head-to-head bouts, so the win over Matsuzaki is a real result, not a bracket artifact. ([jawiki_tokuno](../references.md#jawiki_tokuno))

[^naganuma]: The Naganuma-ha of Jikishinkage-ryū is the same broad school this book otherwise follows, but a distinct sub-line from the Odani-ha / Fujikawa-ha; Tokunō and Kawaji together give the police layer two Naganuma-ha swordsmen, which is worth noting against the assumption that the Keishichō drew only from the sword schools popular in Edo. ([wikipedia-jiki](../references.md#wikipedia-jiki))

[^seppuku]: The suicide is reported consistently — the blade wiped and returned to its sheath before death — but the cause is given as uncertain, most often attributed to the stroke of that spring; the sources leave it unresolved. ([jawiki_tokuno](../references.md#jawiki_tokuno))
