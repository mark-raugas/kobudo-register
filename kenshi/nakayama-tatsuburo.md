Nakayama Tatsusaburō (中山辰三郎) was an important Shindō Yōshin-ryū (神道楊心流) figure[^orth] and the teacher of Ōtsuka Hironori ([大塚博紀](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%A4%A7%E5%A1%9A%E5%8D%9A%E7%B4%80)).[^1] He entered the Shindōkan (神道館) on 10 April 1886. There he learned [Jikishinkage-ryū](../ryuha/jikishinkage-ryu.md) (直心影流) and Shindō Yōshin-ryū, receiving the Shindō Yōshin-ryū betsuden (別伝; supplementary transmission)[^2] from [Matsuoka Katsunosuke](https://www.wadokai.se/kopia-pa-wadoryu-historia) (松岡克之助).[^wado-link]

[^wado-link]: Also [Wado-ryu](https://www.wado-ryu.jp/histor/meiji_01.html) pages and [https://www.shinyokai.com/history.php](https://www.shinyokai.com/history.php).

At age eighteen, his talent recognized, Nakayama entered — on Matsuoka's recommendation — the school of a Jikishinkage-ryū kenjutsu teacher named [Hakomori Yosaburō](hakomori-yosaburo.md) (箱守与三郎祐郷), and five years later received a Jikishinkage-ryū license and became Hakomori's shihan-dai — in time succeeding him at the Genbukan.[^genbukan]

After Matsuoka's death, in the autumn of 1898 Nakayama took up Ono-ha Ittō-ryū (小野派一刀流), receiving a kendō license from [Takano Sasaburō](takano-sasaburo.md) (高野佐三郎) in September 1906[^5] and, on Takano's recommendation, becoming kendō teacher at the old Shimotsuma Middle School. It was much later, in May 1919, that he re-entered the Shindōkan under Inose Motokichi ([猪瀬元吉](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%8C%AA%E7%80%AC%E5%85%83%E5%90%89)) to study Shindō Yōshin-ryū jūjutsu, in order to qualify for a bonesetting license. Sources are not consistent on what license he received from that study.[^6]

Independent [Wadō-lineage sources](https://www.wado-ryu.jp/histor/meiji_01.html) corroborate the jūjutsu side specifically. The Wadō-ryū Renmei history and the Web Hiden profile of Ōtsuka name Nakayama the third-generation head of Shindō Yōshin-ryū jūjutsu (第三世), under whom Ōtsuka trained from April 1905, receiving menkyo kaiden on 1 June 1920 and succeeding as fourth-generation head (第四世). They add that Nakayama transmitted kenjutsu and Iga-ryū (為我流) jūjutsu alongside Shindō Yōshin-ryū, with full transmission — zenden (全伝; complete transmission) — in each.

## End Notes

[^orth]: Orthography: the school is displayed with the willow/tree-radical 楊 (神道楊心流), and was earlier written 新道楊心流; the original used the rarer hand-radical 揚 (神道揚心流) — the form kept in Fujiwara's school history — read as a nod to [Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū](../ryuha/tenjin-shinyo.md) (天神真楊流).

[^1]: The consolidated biography derives entirely from Wadō-/Shindō Yōshin-ryū–side materials — Fujiwara Ryōzō, *Shindō Yōshin-ryū no rekishi to gihō* (神道揚心流の歴史と技法), together with karate and budō periodicals (*Gekkan Karatedō*, *Gekkan Hiden*). The Japanese entry gives the full name as Nakayama Tatsusaburō Yukiyoshi (中山辰三郎行儀) and records a memorial stele (顕彰碑) in Shimotsuma, Ibaraki.

[^2]: Nakayama learned the Shindō Yōshin-ryū betsuden — kodachi-dori (小刀捕), daitō-dori (大刀捕), atemi (当身), kappō (活法) — as the ura side of swordsmanship.

[^genbukan]: The 1915 dictionary names the successor 中山行義, and the Wadō-ryū Renmei gives Nakayama's nanori as 行義 (the Wikipedia entry 行儀 — same reading, Yukiyoshi). The two sources are independent of each other and neither states the identification; it rests on that agreement of the nanori with his standing as Hakomori's shihan-dai.

[^5]: Rendered generically here as a "license." The source describes the 1906 credential with the phrase 特業証書 (kendō tokugyō shōsho in the article), but that phrase is otherwise unattested in indexed Japanese — martial or administrative — and parses only as a generic descriptor ("special-skill certificate"), not a titled license. As it traces to this single source, it is not reproduced as a document type; the actual instrument — an Ono-ha Ittō-ryū license, a Butokukai seirenshō (精錬証) or a Meishinkan (明信館) teaching warrant — remains undetermined.

[^6]: In September 1920 (the September following his May 1919 re-entry) Nakayama received a jūjutsu jitsugi shōmeisho (柔術実技証明書, "jūjutsu practical-skill certificate"), on the basis of which the Ibaraki prefectural office issued the seikotsushi (整骨師, bonesetter) license. The Shindō Yōshin-ryū certificate seems to be the qualifying document; the seikotsushi license was the professional credential. What varies between accounts is mainly whether these two steps are reported separately or collapsed into one event.
