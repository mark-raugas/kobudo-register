Jikishinkage-ryū (直心影流) was the name chosen by
Yamada Heizaemon Ippūsai Mitsunori (山田平左衛門光徳) to refer to his inheritance of the teachings of Takahashi Jikiōsai (直翁斎), whose Jikishin Seitō-ryū he practiced and possibly updated or merged with his practice in a branch of the Edo line of Yagyū Shinkage-ryū.

Jikiōsai had studied the kenjutsu of Kamiya Denshinsai, who was the successor of Ogasawara Genshinsai (said to have refined his art during years spent in Ming China).  Ogasawara was a student of Okuyama Kyūgasai, an early disciple of Kamiizumi Ise-no-kami.

The school's now-familiar Kashima-shinden origin story — Matsumoto Bizen-no-kami as founding ancestor, a dream-transmission from the Kashima deity Takemikazuchi — was a mid-eighteenth-century documentary addition by the eighth head, Ippūsai's successor Naganuma Kunisato.

By the late early-modern period the school had divided into three branches: the **Naganuma-ha** (長沼派), the orthodox trunk that carried the *bōgu*-and-shinai reform; the **Fujikawa-ha** (藤川派); and the **Odani-ha** (男谷派), which embraced competitive matches and became closely tied to the Tokugawa Kōbusho. This chapter follows the Edo *dōtō* of the Odani line — from Fujikawa Yashirōemon Chikayoshi and Akaishi Gunjibei Fusuke through Danno Gennoshin to [Odani Seiichirō](../kenshi/odani-seiichiro.md) — the line that absorbed the Fujikawa house and issued, in the twentieth century, in [Yamada Jirōkichi](../kenshi/yamada-jirokichi.md)'s Seitō-ha.

It was in this line that swordsmanship crossed the Restoration. Odani Seiichirō, foremost swordsman of the Tenpō era, headed the Kōbusho and trained [Sakakibara Kenkichi](../kenshi/sakakibara-kenkichi.md), who — remembered as "the last swordsman" — kept the art before the public in early Meiji through his *gekken-kōgyō* exhibitions; Yamada Jirōkichi preserved and codified that transmission as the Seitō-ha; and, on the Naganuma side, [Tokunō Sekishirō](../kenshi/tokuno-sekishiro.md) carried Jikishinkage-ryū into the Metropolitan Police and became the first recipient of the Butokukai's *hanshi* title. The Edo heads of that Odani line are followed here in succession.

## Fujikawa Yashirōemon Chikayoshi

Fujikawa Yashirōemon Chikayoshi (藤川弥司郎右衛門近義; 1726-1798), the tenth-generation head of Jikishinkage-ryū, whose Edo town-dōjō at Shitaya Chōjamachi (下谷長者町) opened in Hōreki 6 (1756), was reputed to have three thousand students and to have produced Akaishi Gunjibei Fusuke alongside Sakai Ryōsuke ([kotobank-jikishinkage](../references.md#kotobank-jikishinkage)).

Nakabayashi's encyclopedia treatment frames him as the senior disciple of Tsunasato who continued the orthodox Naganuma line. In the dōtō register his formal style is Fujikawa Yashirōemon-no-jō Fujiwara Chikayoshi (藤川弥司郎右衛門尉藤原近義) ([isseikai-dototsu](../references.md#isseikai-dototsu)).

Karukome (2013) details the formation of the Fujikawa-ha, noting that Fujikawa Yashirōemon Chikayoshi entered Naganuma Kunisato's school and then after Kunisato's retirement trained under Naganuma Shōbei. When he died, his adopted son Chikanori took over the dōjō, but died shortly thereafter.

The adopted son-in-law Chikanori married Chikayoshi's daughter, so two young heirs were left after Chikanori's sudden death — Chikatsune and Seisai. They were not yet adult at the time, so Akaishi Gunjibei Fusuke served as guardian to the brothers.[^chikanori]

Fujikawa Seisai (藤川整斎) is the author of *Reiken ryakukai* (1857, Tokyo National Museum) and *Seisai zuihitsu* (整斎随筆, National Archives). Karukome notes that the *Seisai zuihitsu* records, as a kikigaki, a description of a match by Seisai's grandfather Fujikawa Yashirōemon Chikayoshi, through which the character of Fujikawa-ha matches can be glimpsed.

Sakai Ryōsa / Ryōsuke (酒井良佐 / 酒井良佑) was a Fujikawa-ha swordsman whose match is described in Shimizu Rekishū's *Ariya nashiya*, giving the actual line: "Ryōsa, with a great shout, [struck] from jōdan toward the crown of the head…" — i.e. the same upper-stance, striking-from-above style the school is known for. The essay is reprinted in *Zoku Nihon zuihitsu taisei*, vol. 8.[^chi-name]

[^chikanori]: According to Ishigaki, Chikanori was brought in from the Kōno (河野) family of the same domain and married to Chikayoshi's daughter.

[^chi-name]: His given name is written 良佐 by Karukome but 良佑 (glossed Ryōsuke) by Kotobank.

## Akaishi Gunjibei Fusuke

Akaishi Gunjibei (赤石郡司兵衛), honorific *-no-jō* (尉), *honsei* Fujiwara (藤原), imina Fusuke (孚祐), gō Hanryūken (斑龍軒), was the eleventh-generation head of Jikishinkage-ryū in the Kashima-shinden reckoning, standing between Fujikawa Chikayoshi (10th) and Danno Gennoshin (12th) in what became the Odani-ha. His placement is consistent across the school's own dōtō register and the Kobudō Kyōkai succession lists ([isseikai-dototsu](../references.md#isseikai-dototsu); [kobudokyokai-jikishinkage](../references.md#kobudokyokai-jikishinkage)).

He studied under Fujikawa Yashirōemon Chikayoshi, the tenth-generation head ([kotobank-jikishinkage](../references.md#kotobank-jikishinkage)). His recorded students are Danno Gennoshin (団野源之進; the twelfth-generation head), Ishikawa Seheiji (石川瀬平二) ([kotobank-jikishinkage](../references.md#kotobank-jikishinkage)), and Toda Einosuke.

The Jikishinkage-ryū densho-shū (直心影流伝書集) lists Toda Einosuke (imina Ōban Yoshishige〔大判良茂; imina reading uncertain〕, styled Isshinsai) as one of Akaishi's students:

> At the age of twenty-four he came to Kyoto and opened a kōbujō, and his name became greatly renowned. Those who received instruction from him numbered several thousand in all, and those who attained the true transmission were likewise many. He died in Meiji 4 (1871), the kanoto-hitsuji year, in the twelfth month, aged sixty-six.

In Meiji, Ōishi Susumu (大石進), [Okumura Sakonta](../kenshi/okumura-sakonta.md) (奥村左近太), [Takayama Minesaburō](../kenshi/takayama-minesaburo.md) (高山峰三郎), Koteda Yasusada (籠手田安定), and Abe Morie (阿部守衛) later visited the school he founded in Kyoto. Unlike the ninth-generation Naganuma line (Numata-han service), Akaishi sits in the Fujikawa Edo town-dōjō tradition; no domain appointment is attested.

## Danno Gennoshin

Danno Gennoshin (団野源之進; gō Shinpansai 真帆斎)[^reading] was the teacher of Odani Seiichirō; his birth and death are not easily fixed, though Odani inherited his dōjō after Danno's death, c. 1857.

Danno was the twelfth-generation head of Jikishinkage-ryū[^danno-ha] in the Kashima-shinden reckoning that counts from Matsumoto Bizen-no-kami, and the teacher of Odani Nobutomo (男谷信友).[^honsei] His *imina* is given as Yoshitaka (義高), with the variant 義孝 in at least one lineage register.[^imina]

He studied under Akaishi Gunjibei Fusuke (赤石郡司兵衛孚祐), the eleventh-generation head, and maintained a dōjō at Honjo Kamezawa-chō (本所亀沢町) in Edo ([kotobank-jikishinkage](../references.md#kotobank-jikishinkage)). The Kobudō Kyōkai registries place him in the orthodox line between Akaishi and Odani — Akaishi Fusuke — Danno Shinpansai (Yoshitaka) — Odani Seiichirō Nobutomo ([kobudokyokai-jikishinkage](../references.md#kobudokyokai-jikishinkage)).

His principal recorded student is Odani Nobutomo (*tsūshō* Seiichirō, gō Seisai), later "the sword-sage of the Bakumatsu." Odani received the *tekiden* (的伝; the orthodox inner transmission) from Danno in Bunsei 6 (1823) ([kotobank-jikishinkage](../references.md#kotobank-jikishinkage)). On Danno's death Odani was given the Kamezawa-chō dōjō, relocating there in Ansei 5 (1858) — the only indirect anchor available for the period of Danno's death.[^entrydate]

[^reading]: Every Japanese source gives the *tsūshō* reading as Gennoshin (源之進 = げんのしん); the gō is read Shinpansai (真帆斎 = しんぱんさい), an irregular reading of 真帆 confirmed by Kotobank furigana. The kyūjitai form 眞帆斎 also appears in the Kobudō Kyōkai registries. The form "Genshin" is not attested in any source consulted.

[^honsei]: 男谷 is read Odani in the Kotobank headword and in this lineage's house usage; some references (e.g. Japanese Wikipedia) read it Otani. Retained as Odani here.

[^imina]: 義高 in Japanese Wikipedia and the Kobudō Kyōkai pages; 義孝 (same reading) on the Issenkai Seitō page. Both are internal house attestations, not externally corroborated. The formal *honsei* also splits: 藤原 (Fujiwara) in the naginata registry, 源 (Minamoto) in the kenjutsu registry.

[^entrydate]: Odani's date of entry into Danno's school is reported inconsistently: Bunka 2 (1805), age 8, in Japanese Wikipedia and a popular online account; Bunka 14 (1817), with full transmission gained in four years, in Kotobank (Nakabayashi Shinji, Nipponica). These are incompatible sequences and are compounded by the separate dispute over Odani's own birth year (1798 vs 1810). Treated as unresolved.

[^danno-ha]: Japanese Wikipedia, 直心影流剣術, naming Fujikawa-ha (from Fujikawa Chikayoshi), Danno-ha (from Danno Yoshitaka / Shinpansai), and the Odani-ha "among many branches."
